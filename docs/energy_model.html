The Energy Model

Joules per FLOP: fixed overhead + intrinsic compute, and the energy-optimal rate — From GPUs to Joules

A job's total energy splits into two physically distinct parts: energy spent because the machine is on for the duration of the job, and energy spent to actually perform the arithmetic. Both scale with the job's total work, not with the hardware's peak FLOPS.

\[ E_{\text{job}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{F_{\text{job}}}{R_{\text{hw}}}\,P_{\text{fixed}}}_{\text{fixed overhead energy}} \;+\; \underbrace{F_{\text{job}}\,e_{\text{compute}}}_{\text{variable compute energy}} \]

Terms

\(E_{\text{job}}\)
total job energy (J)
\(F_{\text{job}}\)
total computational work (FLOPs)
\(R_{\text{hw}}\)
achieved hardware compute rate (FLOPS = FLOPs/s)
\(P_{\text{fixed}}\)
fixed/base power while the job runs (W = J/s)
\(e_{\text{compute}}\)
variable energy per executed FLOP (J/FLOP)

Derivation

The execution time is the work divided by the achieved rate:

\[ T_{\text{job}} \;=\; \frac{F_{\text{job}}}{R_{\text{hw}}}. \]

So the fixed-overhead energy is just base power integrated over that time,

\[ E_{\text{fixed}} \;=\; T_{\text{job}}\,P_{\text{fixed}} \;=\; \frac{F_{\text{job}}}{R_{\text{hw}}}\,P_{\text{fixed}}, \]

and the variable energy to perform the arithmetic is

\[ E_{\text{compute}} \;=\; F_{\text{job}}\,e_{\text{compute}}. \]

A cleaner form

Factoring out the total work exposes the structure:

\[ E_{\text{job}} \;=\; F_{\text{job}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R_{\text{hw}}} + e_{\text{compute}}\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{E_{\text{job}}}{F_{\text{job}}} \;=\; \frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R_{\text{hw}}} + e_{\text{compute}}. \]

That is joules per FLOP, and it is a sum of two competing terms.

The two terms

The first term, \(\dfrac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R_{\text{hw}}}\), is the fixed power amortized over how much compute the machine does per second. It falls as the hardware runs faster: more FLOPs per second means the always-on base power is spread over more useful work.

The second term, \(e_{\text{compute}}\), is the intrinsic/dynamic energy cost of performing the computation itself.

If \(e_{\text{compute}}\) were constant, faster hardware would always lower total energy:

\[ R_{\text{hw}}\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R_{\text{hw}}}\downarrow . \]

The interesting case: \(e_{\text{compute}}\) rises with rate

In reality the per-FLOP dynamic cost is itself a function of the operating rate, \(e_{\text{compute}} = e_{\text{compute}}(R_{\text{hw}})\), and it typically increases at high performance/power states (higher voltage and frequency cost disproportionately more energy per operation). Then:

\[ E_{\text{job}}(R) \;=\; F_{\text{job}}\left[\frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R} + e_{\text{compute}}(R)\right]. \]

This captures the fundamental tradeoff: going faster reduces fixed-overhead energy but can increase dynamic energy per FLOP. The sum of a decreasing term and an increasing term has a minimum, so there is naturally an energy-optimal compute rate \(R^{\star}\):

\[ \frac{d}{dR}\!\left[\frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{R} + e_{\text{compute}}(R)\right]_{R=R^{\star}} = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad e_{\text{compute}}'(R^{\star}) \;=\; \frac{P_{\text{fixed}}}{{R^{\star}}^{2}}. \]

At \(R^{\star}\) the marginal dynamic cost of running one FLOP/s faster exactly equals the marginal fixed-overhead saving from finishing sooner.

Connection to the measurements

Each point in the sweep (model × GPU × power cap) measures a realized \((R_{\text{hw}}, P)\): the power cap moves the GPU along its \(R\) axis, throughput gives \(R_{\text{hw}}\), and NVML power gives \(P = P_{\text{fixed}} + R_{\text{hw}}\,e_{\text{compute}}(R_{\text{hw}})\). The \(\sim\tfrac12\)TDP driver floor is essentially \(P_{\text{fixed}}\) (leakage + clock tree + idle memory, the part no cap can remove), and the slope of drain vs. rate recovers \(e_{\text{compute}}(R)\). A workload whose \(e_{\text{compute}}(R)\) rises steeply has a low energy-optimal rate; a memory-bound workload, whose rate barely responds to power, sits near \(P_{\text{fixed}}\) with little dynamic term. This per-workload \(R^{\star}\) is precisely what energy-space scheduling allocates around.
